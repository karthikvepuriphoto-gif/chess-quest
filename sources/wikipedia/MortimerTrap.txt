The '''Mortimer Trap''' is a [[chess opening]] [[Chess trap|trap]] in the [[Ruy Lopez]] named after [[James Mortimer (chess player)|James Mortimer]].  The Mortimer Trap is a true trap in the sense that Black deliberately plays an inferior move to tempt White into making a mistake.
{{AN chess|pos=toc}}

==Analysis==
'''1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 Nf6'''
:The trap begins with Black playing the [[Ruy Lopez#Berlin Defence|Berlin Defense]] to the Ruy Lopez. Although the Berlin was much more popular in the 19th century than in the 20th, it "became the height of [[chess theory|theory]] when [[Vladimir Kramnik]] used it as his main defense to defeat [[Garry Kasparov]] in their 2000 [[World Chess Championship|World Championship]] match."{{sfn|de Firmian|2008|p=43}}

'''4. d3'''
:White plays a quiet alternative to the more common 4.0-0, 4.d4, or 4.Nc3 (the last would transpose to the [[Four Knights Game]]). [[I. A. Horowitz]] and [[Fred Reinfeld]] wrote that 4.d3 is "[[Wilhelm Steinitz|Steinitz's]] move, with which he scored many spectacular successes during his long reign as [[World Chess Championship|World Champion]]."{{sfn|Horowitz|Reinfeld|1954|p=59}}

'''4... Ne7{{chesspunc|?!}}'''{{sfn|Matanović|1981|loc=p. 302, n. 2}}
:The Mortimer Defense,{{sfn|Hooper|Whyld|1996|loc=p. 265. ''Mortimer Defence''}} intending to reroute the [[knight (chess)|knight]] to g6. This rare move loses {{chessgloss|time}} and thus is inferior to other moves, but it sets a trap. White has several acceptable replies, for example 5.Bc4 c6 6.Nc3 Ng6 7.h4{{chesspunc|!}} with the upper hand and an attack ([[Paul Keres|Keres]], [[Friðrik Ólafsson|Olafsson]]),{{sfn|Matanović|1981|loc=p. 302, n. 2}} but the tempting capture of the black [[pawn (chess)|pawn]] on e5 is a mistake. 

'''5. Nxe5{{chesspunc|?}} c6!''' (see diagram)
:Attacking the white [[bishop (chess)|bishop]] and threatening 6...Qa5+. If the bishop moves (6.Ba4 or 6.Bc4), Black wins a piece with 6...Qa5+, [[fork (chess)|forking]] the white [[king (chess)|king]] and [[knight (chess)|knight]].

'''6. Nc4{{chesspunc|!?}}'''
:Covering a5 and thus preventing 6...Qa5+, and threatening [[smothered mate]] with 7.Nd6#. 

'''6... Ng6!'''
:After 6...d6 7.Ba4, Black forks the white bishop and knight with 7...b5, winning a piece for two pawns.{{sfn|Hooper|Whyld|1996|loc=p. 265. ''Mortimer Trap''}} Likewise after 6...Ng6, Black will win {{chessgloss|material}}.

==Discussion==
Mortimer played his defense at the [[London 1883 chess tournament|1883 London tournament]] against [[Berthold Englisch]], [[Samuel Rosenthal]], and [[Josef Noa]], losing all three games.{{sfn|Minchin|1973|pp=179, 257, 306}} [[Johannes Zukertort]], the tournament winner, also played it against Englisch, the game resulting in a [[draw (chess)|draw]].<ref>[http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1001912 Englisch–Zukertort]</ref> Zukertort wrote of 4...Ne7, "Mr. Mortimer claims to be the inventor of this move. I adopted it on account of its novelty."{{sfn|Minchin|1973|p=22}} The first edition of the [[treatise]] ''Chess Openings, Ancient and Modern'' analyzed 5.Nc3 Ng6 6.0-0 c6 7.Ba4 d6 8.Bb3 and now the authors gave either 8...Be6 or 8...Be7 as giving Black an equal game.{{sfn|Freeborough|Ranken|1889|p=127}} A bit more recently, Horowitz and Reinfeld observed of 4...Ne7, "This time-wasting retreat of the Knight to an inferior square blocks the development of the King Bishop ... . Yet it is a matter of record that this [[swindle (chess)|pitfall]] had a vogue for many years."{{sfn|Horowitz|Reinfeld|1954|p=59}}
