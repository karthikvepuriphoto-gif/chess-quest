The '''Budapest Gambit''' (or '''Budapest Defence''') is a [[chess opening]] that begins with the moves:

:1. [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. d4|d4]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. d4/1...Nf6|Nf6]]
:2. [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. d4/1...Nf6/2. c4|c4]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. d4/1...Nf6/2. c4/2...e5|e5]]

Black sacrifices a pawn in return for active play. After being introduced by the Hungarian master [[Geza Maroczy]] in an off-hand game in 1896, the Budapest Gambit received attention from leading players after a win as [[White and Black in chess|Black]] by [[Milan Vidmar]] over [[Akiba Rubinstein]] in 1918.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1119780|title=Akiba Rubinstein vs. Milan Vidmar (1918) |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref> It enjoyed some popularity in the early 1920s, but since then it has rarely been played at the top level.

After 3.dxe5 Ng4, intending to recapture the pawn on e5, White has three main replies: 4.Nf3 sees White seeking to return the pawn for quick development; the Alekhine Variation (4.e4) attempts to establish a strong {{chessgloss|pawn centre}} and {{chessgloss|space}} advantage; the Rubinstein Variation (4.Bf4) leads to an important choice for White after 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ (i.e. the reply 6.Nbd2 leads to a positional game in which White often enjoys the {{chessgloss|bishop pair}}, while 6.Nc3 keeps the {{chessgloss|material}} advantage of a pawn but allows Black to weaken White's [[pawn structure]] with ...Bxc3+).

In the less common [[#Fajarowicz Variation 3...Ne4|Fajarowicz Variation]] (3...Ne4), Black forgoes regaining the pawn in favour of quickly generating tactical threats connected with ...Bb4+ as well as the f2-square.

The first use of the opening against a world-class player was at [[Berlin]] in April 1918, a double [[round-robin tournament]] with four players: [[Akiba Rubinstein]], [[Carl Schlechter]], [[Jacques Mieses]] and [[Milan Vidmar]]. Vidmar had to play Black in the first round against Rubinstein, a top-level player with a very {{chessgloss|positional play|positional}} style. At a loss for what to play, he sought advice from his friend Abonyi, who showed him the Budapest Gambit and the main ideas the Hungarian players had found. Vidmar followed Abonyi's advice and beat Rubinstein convincingly in just 24 moves.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 7</ref> This victory so heartened Vidmar that he went on to win the tournament, while Rubinstein was so demoralised by this defeat that he lost another game against Mieses and drew a third one against Schlechter in the same opening.<ref name="Tseitlin8"/><ref>Moskalenko 2007, p. 9</ref>

The leading exponents of 1.d4 started to look for reliable antidotes. [[Alexander Alekhine]] showed how White could get a strong attack with 4.e4 in his games against [[Ilya Rabinovich]] ([[Baden-Baden]] 1925) and [[Adolf Seitz]] ([[Hastings]] 1925–26). But a few weeks later a {{chessgloss|theme tournament}} on the Budapest Gambit was held, in Budapest, and the result was 14½–21½ in Black's favour. Another tournament in [[Semmering, Austria|Semmering]] the same year saw Alekhine losing to [[Karl Gilg]] in his pet line with White against the gambit, so that the e4-line had a mixed reputation.<ref name="Oleinikov_4"/> Meanwhile, more positional plans were also developed for White. Rubinstein showed how White could get a small positional advantage with 4.Bf4 Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2, an assessment still valid today. The possibility 6.Nc3 was also considered attractive, as structural weaknesses were not valued as much as a material advantage of one pawn in those days. By the end of the 1920s, despite the invention of the highly original Fajarowicz Variation 3...Ne4 in 1928, the Budapest Gambit was considered theoretically dubious.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 9</ref>

This assessment was left unchanged for decades, as few players at the highest level used the Budapest Gambit and information about games from lesser players could not easily be found. During that time, various responses were developed against the 4.Bf4 line; these included 4...g5, invented by István Abonyi, further developed by the masters Bakonyi and [[Dolfi Drimer]]. The master Kaposztas showed that even when White succeeded in his positional plan, it only meant for Black a worse endgame with drawish tendencies.<ref group="notes">White's plan involves pawn advances on the queenside, resulting in the creation of a weak pawn for Black, then winning this weak pawn. In this process all minor pieces and queenside pawns are likely to disappear, so that White ends up in a better ending with four pawns on the kingside against three for Black, and only major pieces. This type of ending has drawish tendencies, as Kaposztas demonstrated in his games against Meleghegyi (Budapest 1981), Petran (Budapest 1974) or Farago (Budapest 1975), all of them drawn.</ref> Two pawn sacrifices were also introduced in the variation with 6.Nbd2 (still in the 4.Bf4 line), based on pawn pushes d7–d6 or f7–f6 and a quick attack against b2.<ref name="Oleinikov_6">Oleinikov 2005, chapter 6</ref>

The Budapest Gambit saw a short-lived revival in 1984–85 when ''[[Chess Informant]]'' included three games (as many as in the previous fifteen years), all played at a high level of competition, and all won by Black.<ref name="Oleinikov_7">Oleinikov 2005, chapter 7</ref> But White players found improvements and even invented a line with 4.e3 and 5.Nh3.<ref name="Oleinikov_8">Oleinikov 2005, chapter 8</ref> [[Nigel Short]] attempted to surprise [[Anatoly Karpov]] with this opening in the first game of their [[World Chess Championship 1993|1992 World Championship Candidates semifinal match]], but Karpov won a model game as White.<ref>{{cite news |last=Keene |first=Raymond |author-link=Raymond Keene |date=25 April 1992 |title=Chess |url=https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/25th-april-1992/52/chess |newspaper=The Spectator |page=44 |access-date=17 January 2026}}</ref><ref>Lalic 1998, pp. 23–25</ref> In the 21st century, despite [[Shakhriyar Mamedyarov]]'s successful efforts to rehabilitate the line 4.Bf4 g5, the Budapest Gambit almost never appears at the highest level.<ref name="Oleinikov_12">Oleinikov 2005, chapter 12</ref><ref>Moskalenko 2007, p. 10</ref> [[Vasyl Ivanchuk]] tried it once against [[Levon Aronian]] in the [[2013 Candidates Tournament]], but lost the game.<ref>{{cite web |last=Silver |first=Albert |date=27 March 2013 |title=Candidates R10 – Kramnik, Aronian, Carlsen win |url=https://en.chessbase.com/post/candidates-r10--kramnik-aronian-carlsen-win-280313 |website=ChessBase News |access-date=17 January 2026}}</ref>

==Strategic and tactical themes==

===White builds up an imposing pawn centre===

In the [[#Alekhine Variation 3...Ng4 4.e4|Alekhine Variation]] White does not try to defend the e5-pawn and keep a material advantage, but instead concentrates on building an imposing pawn centre. This brings good prospects of a space advantage that may serve as a basis for a future attack on the {{chessgloss|kingside}}. The extended pawn centre has its drawbacks, however, as Lalic explains: "White must invest some valuable tempi in protecting her pawn structure, which allows Black to seize the best squares for her minor pieces with excellent prospects for {{chessgloss|counterplay}} against the white centre."<ref name="Lalic9">Lalic 1998, p. 9</ref>

Hence in this variation Black lets White build the pawn centre only to undermine it later, a playing philosophy espoused in the teachings of the [[Hypermodernism (chess)|hypermodern school]]. The strategic themes are similar to the ones that can be found in other openings like the [[King's Indian Defence, Four Pawns Attack|Four Pawns Attack of the King's Indian Defence]], the [[Alekhine's Defence|Alekhine Defence]], or the [[Grünfeld Defence]].<ref name="Lalic9"/>

===The "Budapest rook" and Black's kingside attack===
The "Budapest rook" is a manoeuvre in the 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Nf3 Bc5 variation, introduced by Dolfi Drimer in 1968,<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 159</ref> where Black develops the a8 rook aggressively along the sixth {{chessgloss|rank}} using the moves ...a7–a5 and the {{chessgloss|rook lift}} ...Ra8–a6, intending to swing the rook over to the kingside.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 12</ref> For example, this can happen after the further moves 5.e3 Nc6 6.Nc3 0-0 7.Be2 Re8 8.0-0 Nxe5 9.Nxe5 Nxe5 10.b3 a5 11.Bb2 Ra6, when the rook may go to e6, g6 or h6 depending on how White continues.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, pp. 159, 161</ref>

The rook is then used to support a piece attack against White's [[Castling|castled]] king. Black can easily get several pieces around the white king, such as a rook to h6, a queen to h4 and a knight on g4. The queen's arrival on the h4-square is facilitated by the absence of a white knight on the f3-square (that would otherwise cover the h4-square) and of a black knight on the f6-square (that would block the way for the black queen). If White tries to defend with h2–h3, this may allow Black's light-square bishop to be sacrificed at h3 in order to open the h-{{chessgloss|file}}.<ref name="Lalic13">Lalic 1998, p. 13</ref> Black often tries to keep the dark-square bishop on the a7–g1 diagonal, attacking White's pawn on e3 and making it difficult for White to play f2–f4 to chase away the black knight on e5.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 162</ref>

The "Budapest rook" variation became popular in the 1980s. Delaying ...d7–d6 in order to allow the rook lift has its drawbacks: the light-square bishop has to wait a long time to develop, and any attack on the Bc5 is potentially annoying for Black, since it means either closing the sixth rank with ...d6/...b6, abandoning the active a7–g1 diagonal, or blocking the rook if retreated to a7. This, in addition to the committal nature of Black's plan (with nothing to fall back on if the direct attack is repelled), has made some revisit the old lines where Black plays an early ...d7–d6, where it is instead the king's rook that can be brought to the sixth rank via ...Rf8–e8–e6.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 181</ref>

Rapid piece development is a cornerstone of Black's strategy in the Budapest Gambit, and developing the dark-squared bishop to b4 with check is consistent with this theme.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 9<br />Moskalenko 2008, p. 124</ref> The move ...Bb4+ can be followed by ...Qe7 attacking White's pawn on e4 in the Alekhine Variation,<ref>Moskalenko 2008, pp. 125, 131</ref> or on e5 in the Rubinstein Variation.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 24</ref> 

Whenever possible, White tends to block the check by moving the b1-knight to c3 or d2. After White eventually attacks the bishop with a2–a3, Black will be forced to either capture the knight and concede the {{chessgloss|bishop pair}}, or lose time retreating the bishop.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, pp. 50, 52–53</ref>  When choosing the former option, Black will seek some type of positional compensation in return for giving White the bishop pair; otherwise, Black risks drifting into a passive position.<ref>Avrukh 2010, pp. 72, 76–77</ref>

For instance, in the [[#Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4|Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4]] with [[#Line 6.Nc3|4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nc3]], after 6...Bxc3+ 7.bxc3 White's [[Isolated pawn|isolated]], [[Doubled pawns|doubled]] c-pawns provide compensation for Black's loss of the [[bishop pair]], even if Black does not regain the pawn on e5.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 18</ref> Since White cannot control the c5-square with a pawn, that square is useful as an [[Outpost (chess)|outpost]] for Black's pieces.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 47</ref> Piece exchanges can be good for Black even if he is a pawn down, as he can hope to exploit the crippled pawn structure in the ending.<ref name="Lalic10">Lalic 1998, p. 10</ref>

Meanwhile, in the Rubinstein Variation with [[#Line 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.e3|6.Nbd2]], Black can anticipate White's intended a2–a3 by playing ...a7–a5–a4 to fix White's queenside pawns.<ref>Avrukh 2010, p. 72<br />Moskalenko 2008, p. 53</ref> For example, Moskalenko recommends the variation 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.e3 Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.Be2 a5 10.0-0 d6, with the point that if White tries to force Black to trade the b4-bishop with 11.Nb3 followed by 12.a3, Black has the reply 11...a4 12.a3 Bc3! keeping the balance.<ref>Avrukh 2010, p. 82<br />Moskalenko 2008, p. 72</ref>

The move ...Bb4+ can also be effective in the Alekhine Variation. For example, after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.e4 Nxe5 5.f4 Ng6 6.Nf3, the move 6...Bb4+ ({{em|diagram}}) is good because White is forced to reply 7.Nc3. Instead 7.Nbd2{{chesspunc|?}} just loses a pawn after 7...Nxf4, whereas 7.Bd2{{chesspunc|?!}} Qe7{{chesspunc|!}} causes White great problems: both the f4 and e4 pawns are attacked, and 8.Bxb4 Qxb4+ results in a double attack against b2 and f4.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 37</ref> After 7.Nc3 Black can either answer with 7...Bxc3+ 8.bxc3 or with 7...Qf6, simultaneously attacking c3 and f4.<ref group="notes">Examples where ...Bb4+ is weak include the game Döry–[[Savielly Tartakower|Tartakower]] (Vienna 1928), when after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.e3 Nxe5 5.Nf3 Bb4+ White should have played 6.Nbd2 to avoid the exchange of bishops and gain a tempo later with a2–a3, with a small plus (see Tseitlin 1992, p. 13).<br>Similarly, after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Nf3, Black should not play 4...Bb4+ because White can answer 5.Nbd2! Nc6 6.e3 Ngxe5 7.Nxe5 Nxe5 8.a3! Bxd2+ 9.Qxd2 and White has the better prospects. He has the bishop pair and he can develop his Bc1 on the influential a1–h8 diagonal (see Tseitlin 1992, p. 69).</ref>

===White's f-pawn advance===
An important idea for White to generate a kingside [[Initiative (chess)|initiative]] in many lines of the Budapest is to advance the f-pawn. To begin with, the f2–f4 advance drives away Black's centralized knight on e5. Black then has to be careful not to allow White an opportune moment to advance the pawn to f5 or even f6.<ref>Avrukh 2010, pp. 80, 86, 89<br />Georgiev 2010, pp. 127, 130–131, 139<br />Martin 2021, p. 166<br />Moskalenko 2008, p. 120</ref> As discussed below, instances of this idea occur in the [[#Line 5...Ng6|Alekhine Variation with 5...Ng6]] and Spassky's plan in the [[#Line 4...Bc5 without a2–a3|4.Nf3 Bc5 5.e3 variation]].

In the latter variation, when White plays f2–f4, the e4-square is weakened. In response, Black can seek counterplay by applying pressure on the e4-square with the manoeuvre ...Ne5–d7–f6 in combination with a rook on e8.<ref name="Lalic81">Lalic 1998, p. 81</ref>

In the main lines the pawn push c4–c5 often brings positional gains to White. In the [[#Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4|Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4]] with [[#Line 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3|4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3]], after 7...Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.e3 Bxd2+ 10.Qxd2 ({{em|see diagram}}) White gets the [[bishop pair]] and a space advantage. In order to build up on these potential advantages, the most common plan is to perform a {{chessgloss|minority attack}} on the queenside, with the goal of performing the pawn advance c4–c5 in favourable conditions.<ref name="Moskalenko54">Moskalenko 2008, p. 54</ref> This push can yield several advantages to White: it enhances the prospects of the {{chessgloss|light-square bishop}}, it creates a [[half-open file]] to attack with the rooks, and it creates an [[Isolated pawn|isolated]], [[backward pawn]] on d6 after the exchange c5xd6.<ref name="Moskalenko54"/>

For example, in the adjacent diagram, after the natural but mistaken 10...0-0?! White can immediately realise his strategic goal with 11.c5!<ref name="Lalic32">Lalic 1998, p. 32<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1398694
 | title=Gurevich–Miezis, Bad Godesburg 1996
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> Then if Black accepts the temporary sacrifice after 11...Qxc5 12.Rc1 Qd6 13.Qxd6 cxd6 14.Rd1 White gets his pawn back and has created a weak pawn in d7, while if Black declines the pawn he has difficulties in developing his queenside (for example 11...d6 might be followed by 12.cxd6 Qxd6 13.Qxd6 cxd6 and the pawn on d6 is weak).<ref name="Lalic32"/> Therefore, Black generally tries to hinder the c4–c5 push with moves like d7–d6, b7–b6 or Rf8–d8 (if this creates a hidden ''vis-à-vis'' between the Rd8 and the Qd2).<ref name="Moskalenko54"/>

Similarly, in the [[#Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4|Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4]] with [[#Line 6.Nc3|4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nc3]], after 6...Bxc3+ 7.bxc3 White is saddled with doubled pawns in c3 and c4 that limit the scope of his bishop pair. Hence the push c4–c5 can be used to free the light-squared bishop and disrupt Black's position.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 10<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1308821
 | title=Gligorić–Westerinen, Venice 1971
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref>

In the [[#Line 4...Bc5 without a2–a3|variation 3...Ng4 4.Nf3 Bc5]], White can sometimes use the c4–c5 push to restrict the range of Black's dark-squared bishop.<ref>Georgiev 2010, pp. 143–144<br />Lalic 1998, p. 79<br />Martin 2021, p. 167</ref> For instance, after 5.e3 Nc6 6.Nc3 0-0 7.Be2 Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.0-0 Re8 10.b3 a5, [[Boris Avrukh]] recommends 11.Na4, intending to meet 11...Ba7 with 12.c5.<ref>Avrukh 2010, p. 88</ref>

The Kieninger Trap is named after [[Georg Kieninger]] who used it in an offhand game against Godai at Vienna in 1925.<ref name="Borik24">Borik 1986, p. 24</ref> It occurs in the [[#Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4|Rubinstein Variation 3...Ng4 4.Bf4]] with [[#Line 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3|4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3]]. White attacks the bishop on b4, but instead of moving it, Black can regain the gambit pawn and set a {{chessgloss|trap}} with 7...Ngxe5 ({{em|see diagram}}). White cannot capture the bishop with 8.axb4{{chesspunc|??}} because Black would respond with the [[smothered mate]] 8...Nd3#; the same is true if White first exchanges knights on e5.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 58</ref>

A similar tactic has also occurred in a {{chessgloss|miniature}} in the Fajarowicz Variation, after the moves 1.d4&nbsp;Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ne4 4.Qc2 Bb4+ 5.Nd2 d5 6.exd6 Bf5 7.Qa4+ Nc6 8.a3 Nc5 9.dxc7&nbsp;Qe7! when White, trying to save his queen, fell into 10.Qd1 Nd3 mate.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 82<br>[[Khelil Lagha|Lagha]]–[[Ennio Contedini|Contedini]], [[14th Chess Olympiad|Leipzig Olympiad]], 1960</ref>
{{clear}}

==3...Ng4 4.Nf3==
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Nf3''
{{Chess diagram small

By playing the natural 4.Nf3, White is ready to return the e5-pawn in order to focus on quick development. White's positional goals include control of the d5-square and the a1–h8 diagonal, and expansion on either the queenside or kingside as appropriate with the pawn pushes c4–c5 and f2–f4–f5–f6 respectively.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 68<br />Georgiev 2010, pp. 130–131<br />Martin 2021, p. 166</ref>

Black can try the minor line 4...Nc6 that delays the development of its {{chessgloss|dark-square bishop}}, to develop it along the a1–h8 diagonal instead of the a3–f8 diagonal, depending on the circumstances. But the main line is 4...Bc5 to attack the f2-pawn, forcing 5.e3, blocking in White's bishop on c1, so that after 5...Nc6 White will not have enough pieces to protect his e5-pawn in the long run. Placing the bishop on the c5-square also has subtler points, as Tseitlin explains:<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 70</ref><blockquote>At first sight the bishop on c5 lacks prospects, being held at bay by the pawn on e3, and is insecure in view of the threat to exchange it by Nc3–a4/e4. In reality, posting the bishop here has a deep strategic significance. It holds up the advance of the e- and the f-pawns (assuming the white bishop will go to b2), and thereby secures e5 as a future knight outpost, which in turn restricts the activity of both White's bishops. As to the exchanging threat, the bishop may conveniently retreat on a7 or f8, or even in some cases remain on c5 with support from a pawn on b6.</blockquote>

An important theoretical decision for White is to choose whether to play a2–a3. While this move protects the b4 square and threatens the pawn advance b2–b4, it encourages Black's rook lift Ra8–a6–h6. As Lalic puts it:<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 82</ref><blockquote>It was not so long ago that 8.a3, with the obvious intention of expanding with b2–b4, was the standard move. However, after Black responds with the logical a7–a5, it became apparent in tournament practice that the inclusion of these moves is in fact in Black's favour, as it gives his queen's rook access into play via the a6-square.</blockquote>

===Line 4...Bc5 with a2–a3===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Nf3 Bc5 5.e3 Nc6 6.a3''

The opinion of the move 6.a3 has gradually shifted from being the main continuation to being a possible continuation, then down to its present status of being considered a mistake. The threat to push b2–b4 must be taken seriously by Black, who typically answers 6...a5. But in the 1980s it was discovered that the push a7–a5 was actually a very useful one for Black, as it allows the Ra8 to be developed along the sixth rank. Meanwhile, the push a2–a3 is less useful for White, as he will not be able to easily push b2–b4. As Tseitlin puts it, "the point is that 6...a5 fits into the plan of attacking White's kingside, whereas 6.a3 does little in the way of defending it".<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 83</ref> Thus if White does not find a clear way to make good use of his move a2–a3, it may turn out to be a critical waste of [[tempo (chess)|tempo]].<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 87</ref><ref name="Silman">Silman 2002</ref>

After the topical moves 7.b3 0-0 8.Bb2 Re8 9.Nc3 Ngxe5 10.Nxe5 Nxe5 11.Be2 Black has regained the invested pawn. White has a space advantage in the centre and can initiate pressure here or on the queenside by pawn pushes like b3–b4 and c4–c5 (possibly supported by a knight on the d5-square). Meanwhile, the white king lacks defenders so Black can start a pieces-driven attack with the {{chessgloss|rook lift}} 11...Ra6 (see {{seclink|#Budapest rook}}). The stem game continued with 12.Nd5 Rh6 13.Bd4 d6 14.Ra2 Bf5 15.Bxc5 dxc5 and Black won in 26 moves.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 87<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1466304
 | title=Arencibia vs. Motwani, Dubai Olympiad 1986
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> To avoid such an unfavourable development, White players have changed the move order to keep the Bc1 on its original square as long as possible, so that it can help the defence. Thus, the typical move order became 7.b3 0-0 8.Nc3 Re8 9.Be2 Ngxe5 10.Nxe5 Nxe5 11.0-0 when 11...Ra6 would be met with 12.Nd5 Rh6 13.e4 immediately attacking the maverick rook. So Black usually opts for 11...d6, forgetting about the Ra8–a6–h6 manoeuvre. After 12.Bb2 ''ECO'' considers the situation as favourable to White, but Tseitlin thinks Black still has a lot of possibilities (e.g. the other rook lift Re8–e6–h6), so that "the struggle still lies ahead".<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 88</ref>

===Line 4...Bc5 without a2–a3===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Nf3 Bc5 5.e3 Nc6''
{{Chess diagram small
| floatright
|
|rd|  |bd|qd|rd|  |kd|
|pd|pd|pd|pd|  |pd|pd|pd
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

By refraining from the advance a2–a3 White tries to gain a tempo on the lines of the previous section, making it more difficult for Black to initiate the Re8–e6–h6 or Ra8–a6–h6 lifts. After the moves 6.Be2 0-0 7.0-0 Re8 8.Nc3 Ngxe5 9.Nxe5 Nxe5 White has tried two different plans.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 159</ref>

The older one sees White attack in the centre with moves like b2–b3, Bc1–b2, Qd1–d5, Nc3–e4 and c4–c5. White gets an important space advantage in the centre, but Black can attack the kingside with rook lifts. After 10.b3 a5 White can try to capture the Bc5 with 11.Na4 or 11.Ne4, one point being that the retreat 11...Ba7 would lock the Ra8 because Black has not played ...Ra8–a6 already. Lalic still thinks 11...Ba7 is the right move after 11.Ne4 due to the importance of the a7–g1 diagonal, but Black can also reroute the bishop with 11...Bf8 and "White has no obvious path to even a minute advantage".<ref name="Lalic79">Lalic 1998, p. 79</ref> Avrukh recommends 11.Na4 intending to meet 11...Ba7 with 12.c5, and 11...Bf8 with 12.f4.<ref>Avrukh 2010, pp. 88–89</ref> The most common continuation is 11.Bb2, keeping the knight jumps for later. Then the most common plan for Black is a rook lift: the plan ...Ra8–a6–h6 was tried in the much-commented game [[Ralf Åkesson|Åkesson]]–Tagnon (Berlin Open 1984). Black duly won, but after the game continuation 11...Ra6 12.Qd5! Qe7 13.Ne4 Ba7 14.c5 Rg6 15.Rac1 Bb8 16.f4 authors do not agree on which side had the advantage. Borik and Tseitlin both consider White to have a positional advantage, with Tseitlin recommending instead 15...Nc6!, with dangerous threats.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 17</ref><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 80</ref> However Lalic writes of 15...Bb8, "it is true that the bishop pair looks a bit pathetic lined up on the back rank just now, but there is no way to stop them breaking out later".<ref name="Lalic76">Lalic 1998, p. 76</ref>

The second plan for White, unveiled by [[Boris Spassky|Spassky]] in 1990, aims at a kingside [[blitzkrieg]] with moves like Kg1–h1, f2–f4, Be2–d3 and Qd1–h5. In the original game Black did not fathom White's idea, so that after 10.Kh1 a5?! 11.f4 Nc6 12.Bd3 d6 13.Qh5! h6 14.Rf3 Black's pieces were ill-placed to counter White's attack.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 132<br>{{cite web 
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1059446
 | title=Spassky vs. Illescas, Linares 1990
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> A more principled plan for Black is to react in the centre, specifically targeting the backward e3-pawn and e4-square. After 10.Kh1 d6 11.f4 Nd7! 12.Bd3 Nf6 13.Qf3 Ng4 14.Nd1 f5! and Black has succeeded in inhibiting White's e3–e4 expansion .<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 81<br>[http://www.chess.com/games/view.html?id=4134301 Alekseev–Bliumberg, Minsk 1993]</ref> As Black was doing fine with the 11.f4 move order, White has been searching for a new path with 10.Kh1 d6 11.Na4{{chesspunc|!?}} b6! 12.Bd2 a5 13.Nxc5 bxc5 14.f4 Nd7 15.Bf3 when [[Jeremy Silman]] prefers White.<ref>Silman 2002<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1483686
 | title=Beliavsky vs. Mohr, Portoroz 1997
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> White has even dared the immediate 10.f4 Nc6 11.Bd3 when it is extremely dangerous for Black to take the offered e3-pawn, as White gets a fierce kingside attack for free.<ref name="Silman"/>
{{clear}}

==Rubinstein Variation: 3...Ng4 4.Bf4==
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Bf4''
{{Chess diagram small

This move sequence is called the "Rubinstein Variation" in reference to the famous game [[Akiba Rubinstein|Rubinstein]]–[[Milan Vidmar|Vidmar]] (Berlin 1918) when 4.Bf4 was first employed.<ref name="Moskalenko15">Moskalenko 2007, p. 15</ref> Various authors consider this move to be the most dangerous for Black.<ref name="Silman"/> It aims to answer 4...Bc5 with 5.e3 without blocking the Bc1, contrary to what happens in the 4.Nf3 variation.<ref name="Moskalenko15"/>

Apart from the sideline 4...g5, the main line continues with both players developing their pieces around the e5-pawn with 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ when White has an important choice between the moves 6.Nc3 and 6.Nbd2, each leading to extremely different play. With 6.Nc3 White acquiesces to the breakup of his queenside pawns in return for a material advantage of one pawn, the bishop pair and active play in the centre. With 6.Nbd2 White gives back the gambited pawn to keep a healthy pawn structure and acquire the [[bishop pair]]. After 6.Nbd2&nbsp;Qe7 White generally plays 7.a3 to force the immediate exchange of bishop for knight, gaining the bishop pair, a spatial advantage and chances for a minority attack on the queenside. White can also try 6.Nbd2&nbsp;Qe7 7.e3 to win a tempo over the 7.a3 variation, though he may end up with the exchange at d2 made in less favourable circumstances, or not at all. The maverick gambit 6...f6 also exists, which Rubinstein attributed to [[Ernst Grünfeld]].<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 126<br>{{cite web| url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1004153| title=Rubinstein vs. Tartakower, Bad Kissingen 1928| website=[[Chessgames.com]]}}</ref>

===Sideline 4...g5===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Bf4 g5''
{{Chess diagram small
| floatright

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |

The sideline 4...g5!? was not well regarded until the end of the 20th century.<ref group="notes">Borik wrote that "the move 4...g5 creates irreparable weaknesses in Black's camp" (see Borik 1986, p. 22), while Tseitlin decided "this extravagant tactical stroke weakens the kingside and, on general grounds alone, cannot be good" (see Tseitlin 1992, p. 41). Lalic warned that "Black should be aware of the risks he is taking by playing such a line" (see Lalic 1998, p. 65).</ref> It weakens several squares—particularly f5 and h5—as they cannot be covered by the g-pawn any more. White can try to exploit these weaknesses with the manoeuvres Bf4–d2–c3 (pressure along the diagonal a1–h8), Ng1–e2–g3–h5 (pressure against the squares f6 and g7) and h2–h4 (to open the h-file). Nonetheless, the 4...g5 line has found new supporters in recent years thanks to black wins against both 5.Bg3 and 5.Bd2.<ref>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1487255
 | title=Van Wely vs. Mamedyarov, Ciudad Real 2004 |website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref><ref>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1479186
 | title=Graf vs. Asik, Kavala 2007 |website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref>

For years, the reaction 5.Bg3 was not well considered, because the retreat does not make the most out of Black's provocative fourth move; as Tseitlin points out, "the bishop is in danger of staying out of play for a long time".<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 41</ref> But later Lalic found that 5.Bg3 was "just as effective" as 5.Bd2.<ref name="Lalic 1998, p. 65">Lalic 1998, p. 65</ref> Black concentrates on capturing the e5-pawn while White tries to get an advantage from the weakening of the black kingside. After the typical moves 5...Bg7 6.Nf3 Nc6 7.Nc3 Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.e3 d6 Lalic considers the best try to be 10.c5!, sacrificing a pawn to weaken Black's control on the e5-square and expose the black king further. White has also tried to quickly open the h-file with 7.h4 Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.e3 but after 9...g4! Black succeeds in keeping the file closed.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 67<br>Amura–Paglilla, Buenos Aires Open 1995</ref>

The alternative to 5.Bg3 is 5.Bd2 to place the bishop on the wide-open diagonal a1–h8, after which "White can expect a safe advantage".<ref name="Lalic 1998, p. 65"/> Then according to Lalic, delaying the recapture with 5...Bg7 6.Bc3 Nc6 7.e3 Ngxe5 is not correct as White can gain an advantage by 8.h4 or 8.Qh5,<ref name="Lalic66">Lalic 1998, p. 66<br>Kuraszkiewicz–Bartsch, Germany 1996</ref> so the immediate 5...Nxe5 is better. For some time 6.Bc3 was well considered because Black had problems dealing with various positional threats, but the correct way for Black was found in 5...Nxe5 6.Bc3 Qe7 7.e3 Rg8! 8.Nf3 Nbc6 9.Be2 d6 10.Nd4 Bd7 11.b4 g4 with good counterplay for Black on the kingside.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 66<br>Elbilia–Bartsch, Cannes Open 1995</ref> White's efforts then switched to 6.Nf3 to open the e-file, something that Black cannot really avoid, as 6...Bg7 7.Nxe5 Bxe5 8.Bc3 would leave an advantage to White.<ref name="Lalic66"/> For example, 8...Qe7 9.Bxe5 Qxe5 10.Nc3 d6 11.e3 and Black is at a loss for an equalising line,<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 47</ref> White's advantage consisting in his ability to install his knight on the strong d5-square and to attack the weakened Black's kingside with the advance h2–h4. It is better for Black to continue with 6...Nxf3+ 7.exf3 when both 7...h5? and 7...Bg7 would fail to 8.Qe2+, so Black must try 7...d6 8.Qe2+ Be6 instead.<ref name="Lalic66"/>
{{clear}}

This is the only important line in the Budapest where Black is not ensured of regaining his sacrificed pawn. Black does best to immediately exchange the Nc3 with 6...Bxc3+ 7.bxc3 as otherwise White gets a small positional advantage simply by avoiding the doubled pawns (see {{seclink|#Advantages of ...Bb4+}}).<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 51<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1083465
 | title=Korchnoi vs. Gomez Esteban, Pamplona 1990–91
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 49</ref> Then Black can put pressure on the e5-pawn with 7...Qe7 when White's only possibility to keep the pawn is 8.Qd5. White threatens to ease the pressure with the move h2–h3 that would force the Ng4 to the unfavourable square h6, so Black's only possibilities to sustain the initiative are 8...Qa3 and 8...f6.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 50</ref>

The line 8...Qa3 puts pressure on the white queenside pawns, pressure that may later be intensified with Nf6–e4. The black queen also gains access to the a5-square, from where it puts pressure on the e1–a5 diagonal aimed towards the white king. After 9.Rc1 f6 10.exf6&nbsp;Nxf6 11.Qd2 d6 12.Nd4 0-0 we reach the position of the famous game between Rubinstein and Vidmar, when Rubinstein erred with 13.e3? and later lost.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 7<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1119780
 | title=Rubinstein vs. Vidmar, Berlin 1918
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> After the better 13.f3 the correct method for Black is to target the c4-pawn with the regrouping Ne5/Qc5.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 55</ref> Hence Lalic thinks 11.Qd2 is inappropriate and gives Black excellent counterplay, and prefers 11.Qd3 or even 11.Qd1!? After 11.Qd3 0-0 12.g3 d6 13.Bg2 Black should switch to a materialistic mode with 13...Qxa2.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 55<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1147689
 | title=van Wely vs. Sorin, Buenos Aires 1995
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}<br>Yakovich–Coret, Seville 1992</ref>

In the other line 8...f6 Black does not want to decentralise his queen and prefers to concentrate on active piece play in the centre. After 9.exf6 Nxf6, 10.Qd1, 10.Qd2 and 10.Qd3 are all possible, but each has its drawbacks: on d1 the queen is not developed, on d3 it is exposed to ...Bc8–f5 and on d2 it is exposed to ...Nf6–e4. Lalic considers 10.Qd3 to be the main move, qualifies 10.Qd1 as a "respectable option", but considers 10.Qd2 as "inaccurate". Meanwhile, Black will try to create counterplay by attacking either the weak c4-pawn, or the kingside with ...g7–g5 and ...h7–h5. In both cases a key possibility is the move ...Nf6–e4 that centralises the knight, attacks the weak c3-pawn, controls the c5-square and supports the ...g7–g5 thrust.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 51<br>Moskalenko 2007, p. 26</ref>

===Line 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3===

====On the way till 10...d6====
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Bf4 Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.a3''
{{Chess diagram small
 |floatright
 |
 |rd|  |bd|  |kd|  |  |rd
 |pd|pd|pd|pd|qd|pd|pd|pd
 |  |  |nd|  |  |  |  |
 |  |  |  |  |pl|  |  |
 |  |bd|pl|  |  |bl|nd|
 |pl|  |  |  |  |nl|  |
 |  |pl|  |nl|pl|pl|pl|pl
 |rl|  |  |ql|kl|bl|  |rl
 |After 7.a3 White will win the bishop pair.

The Bb4 is attacked but Black can play 7...Ngxe5 to get the gambitted pawn back, as 8.axb4?? would allow the Kieninger trap 8...Nd3 mate (see {{seclink|#Kieninger trap}}). Now White is more or less forced to exchange a pair of knights with 8.Nxe5 Nxe5.<ref group="notes">Black threatens both the c4-pawn and the Nf3, and 8.e3?! Nxf3+ forces either 9.gxf3 with doubled pawns or 9.Qxf3 Bxd2+ 10.Kxd2, when White cannot castle any more. White does not want to play 8.Bxe5?! either because it would cede the bishop pair, which is the main source of White's hopes for an advantage in this line.</ref> White still cannot win a piece with 9.axb4?? Nd3# or 9.Bxe5?! Bxd2+ 10.Qxd2 Qxe5, so he usually plays 9.e3 in order to protect the c4-pawn and defuse the mating threat, so that now Black is obliged to move his Bb4. As 9...Bd6 would misplace the bishop and 9...Ba5?? would lose the bishop to 10.b4 Bb6 11.c5, Black usually plays 9...Bxd2+ 10.Qxd2.<ref group="notes">Note that for Black, the sequence 7...Ngxe5 8...Nxe5 9...Bxd2+ is not only cunning, but also the best move order as another sequence would give White an early opportunity to realise the advantageous c4–c5 push (whose advantages are explained in the section "[[Budapest Gambit#Breakthrough with the c4–c5 push|Breakthrough with the c4–c5 push]]"). For example after 7...Bxd2+?! 8.Qxd2 Ngxe5 9.Nxe5 Nxe5 White should not play the usual 10.e3?! but should strive for more with the immediate 10.c5! as Black cannot take in c5 without losing the c7-pawn because of the possibilities Ra1–c1 and Qd2–c3 (see Lalic 1998, p. 33).</ref>

After 10.Qxd2, Tseitlin explains, "Opening manuals assess this position as favourable to White on the basis of the bishop pair. However, considering the closed nature of the position, White faces substantial difficulties in the realisation of this nominal advantage."<ref name="Tseitlin63">Tseitlin 1992, p. 63</ref> White will try a {{chessgloss|minority attack}} on the queenside, in order to increase its space advantage and to create some weaknesses in the black pawns (e.g. an [[isolated pawn]] or a [[backward pawn]]). So White will try to use the advances b2–b4 or c4–c5 in good conditions, supported by the queen and the rooks on the c-file and the d-file. On the other hand, Black will try to keep the position closed, most importantly by keeping the c4-pawn where it is in order to keep the Bf1 at bay. This can be achieved by moves like ...b7–b6 and ...d7–d6, and sometimes the manoeuvre ...Ne5–d7–f8–e6. The first move by Black has to be 10...d6! because otherwise White plays 11.c5! and gets a clear advantage immediately. For example, 10...b6? loses a pawn to 11.Qd5 Nc6 12.Bxc7, and 10...0-0?! is bad because of 11.c5! Qxc5? 12.Rc1 Qe7 13.Rxc7 and White is winning already.<ref name="Lalic32"/>

International Master Timothy Taylor has suggested an alternative for Black on move nine. He regards 9...Bxd2+ as inferior, arguing that "the strong black bishop is traded for the inoffensive knight, and White gets the long-term advantage of the two bishops in a semi-open game".<ref>Taylor 2009, p. 161.</ref> Taylor instead advocates 9...Bc5, when Black stands well after 10.b4 Bd4! (11.exd4?? Nd3#) 11.Rb1 d6 12.Be2 Bf5 13.Rb3 Ng6 14.Bg3 (14.exd4 Nxf4 15.Re3? Nxg2+ wins; 14.Bxd6 cxd6 15.exd4 Nf4 16.g3 Bc2! wins material) Bf6; 10.Ne4 Ng6; 10.Nb3 Bd6; or 10.Be2 d6.<ref>Taylor 2009, pp.164–67</ref>

After 10...d6! White can try (and has tried) about any move that supports the aforementioned plan. In particular, White has to choose if he wants to start active operations on the queenside immediately (e.g. Rc1, Qc3, c5), or if he wants to finish his development first (with Be2 and 0-0). The immediate 11.c5!? is a possible pawn sacrifice in order to open some diagonals for the bishops. As Lalic points out, "after 11...dxc5 Black's knight on e5 has lost its support and therefore all tactical motifs based on Qd5 and Bb5+ must be carefully checked".<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 36</ref> White gets a powerful attack for his pawn but nothing decisive. The same idea can be tried with the preparatory 11.Rc1, and after 11...0-0 12.c5!? dxc5 13.Qd5 Ng6 14.Bg3 White should be reminded that he has not finished his development with 14...Qf6! and a counterattack on the b2-pawn.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 64</ref><ref>Lalic 1998, p. 37</ref> Playing Black, [[Peter Svidler|Svidler]] chose a different path with 11...b6 but his opponent [[Alexandre Lesiège|Lesiège]] nevertheless sacrificed the pawn with 12.c5! bxc5 13.b4 0-0 14.bxc5 Bb7 15.f3 and Svilder chose to destroy his own pawn structure with 15...dxc5!? to activate his pieces and make use of the d-file.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 38<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1136197
 | title=Lesiège vs. Svidler, Oakham 1992
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> The most popular move is 11.Be2, where White castles before beginning queenside play. This gives Black more time to organise a defence on the queenside with ...b7–b6, either now or after 11...0-0.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 38</ref>

===Line 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.e3===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.Bf4 Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.e3''
{{Chess diagram small
| floatright
|
|rd|  |bd|  |kd|  |  |rd
|pd|pd|pd|pd|qd|pd|pd|pd
|  |  |nd|  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |pl|  |  |

|  |  |  |  |pl|nl|  |

In this variation White tries to avoid the move a2–a3 in order to gain a tempo over the 7.a3 variation. After the standard moves 7...Ngxe5 8.Nxe5 Nxe5 9.Be2 followed by 10.0-0 Black must decide whether or not to exchange the Bb4 for the Nd2.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 15</ref>

Lalic thinks the strategies in which Black gives up the bishop pair (by exchanging its Bb4 for the Nd2) for nothing are a mistake. He does not like the strategy to retreat the Bb4 in d6 either, because they are too drawish. He recommends the strategy to retreat the bishop in c5, and maintain its position there with the help of the a7–a5 pawn advance.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 30</ref>

====Black gives up the bishop pair====
When Black opts for 10...Bxd2, he runs the risk to end up a tempo down over the 7.a3 variation and to be soon unable to meet White's positional threats on the queenside. White can avoid the push a2–a3 and continue with the standard plans of the 7.a3 variation.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 24<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1117113
 | title=Garcia Palermo vs. Rogers, Reggio Emilia 1984–85
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> Everything is not that bad for Black, however. First, to implement his plan White has to concentrate on development (9.Be2, 10.0-0) before he turns his attention to the queenside. That means Black has more time to organise his play than in the 7.a3 variation, notably to attempt a blockade of the c5-square. Moreover, as White does not put immediate pressure on Black's position, Black is not compelled to castle rapidly and he can keep his king in the centre for a longer time, or even castle queenside. Hence Lalic note that "White has not wasted time with a2–a3, but in fact it is not so easy to capitalise on this extra tempo."<ref name="Lalic17">Lalic 1998, p. 17</ref>

A possibility for Black is to develop his light-square bishop rapidly, by prioritising the moves b7–b6 and Bc8–b7 over castling and d7–d6. The game Solozhenkin–Stiazhkin (Leningrad 1990) continued with 9...b6 10.0-0 Bxd2 11.Qxd2 Bb7 12.c5 bxc5 13.Qa5 d6 14.Bxe5 dxe5 15.Rfc1 and Moskalenko assesses this position as better for White;<ref>Moskalenko 2007, p. 69</ref> Lalic suggests that 13...Ng6 is an improvement.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 18</ref> In the game [[Einar Gausel|Gausel]]–Reite (Norwegian Team Championship 1991), after the same 9.Be2 b6 10.0-0 Bxd2 11.Qxd2 Bb7 Black introduced a highly original plan by avoiding the natural advance d7–d6, and instead blocked a white c5-push by playing ...c5 himself. The game continued 12.Qc3 f6 13.b4 c5!? and Lalic was "deeply impressed by this plan, which really spoils all of White's fun". The c4-pawn is never allowed to advance, so that the Be2 is durably restricted. The Bf4 is obstructed by the Ne5, that cannot be easily removed. The weakness of the d7-pawn is not a worry as it can be protected by Bb7–c6 if necessary.<ref name="Lalic17"/>

====Black keeps the bishop pair====

After 9.Be2 0-0 10.0-0 Black can avoid the immediate exchange of his Bb4 against the Nd2 in several ways. The first one, resurrected and elaborated by the grandmaster [[Pavel Blatny]], is to exchange the Bb4 for the Bf4. This can be achieved via 10...Ng6 11.Bg3 (11.Bxc7?? d6 loses a piece) 11...Bd6 12.Bxd6 Qxd6. White still has possibilities to play for an advantage due to his more advanced development, his space advantage on the queenside and the possibility to install his knight on the good square d5. Taylor considers this Black's best line, stating that Black has not given White the bishop pair, nor weakened his pawn structure, and should be able to gradually equalize.<ref>Taylor 2009, p. 154</ref>

The other possibility for Black is to keep his Bb4 as long as possible, exchanging it against the white knight only in favourable circumstances. A couple of attempts have been done with this in mind, with subtle variations along the moves a7–a5, b7–b6 and d7–d6. Against the mundane 10...d6 White can continue with 11.Nb3 ({{em|diagram}}) to play on the queenside against the exposed Bb4, or 11.Nb1 to recycle the knight on the ideal d5-square. Another idea is the immediate 10...a5, to have the d6-square for the bishop, inhibit the b2–b4 push and have the possible a5–a4 pawn advance if the white knight moves to b3. In the game Mikhalevski–Chabanon (Bad Endbach 1995)<ref>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1487251
 | title=Mikhalevski vs. Chabanon, Bad Endbach 1995 |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref> Black kept the bishop with 11.Nb3 a4 12.a3&nbsp;Bd6 13.Nd4 Bc5 14.Nb5 d6 15.Nc3 Ng6 16.Bg3 f5 and had dynamic play.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 27</ref>

|  |  |  |pd|  |  |  |

With 4...Bb4+ 5.Nbd2 d6 ({{em|diagram}}) Black wants to open the diagonal a1–h8 for his queen. After 6.exd6 Qf6 White can react to the attack on his Bf4 in several ways, the best one being 7.Nh3 to develop a piece and protect both the Bf4 and the f2-pawn. It also helps that the Bf4 is still guarding the Nd2, so that after 7...Qxb2? there is not the threat of winning the exchange (8...Bxd2+ would be answered by 9.Bxd2) and White can repel Black's attack with 8.Rb1 Qa3 9.Rb3 Qa5 10.dxc7 Nc6 11.a3! Be7 12.e3.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 61<br>[[Evgeny Gleizerov|Gleizerov]]–Ritova, Berlin 1996</ref> Instead, Black must play energetically with 7...Nxf2 8.Kxf2 Bxh3 9.g3 Bxf1 10.dxc7!? Nc6 11.Rxf1 and here Lalic recommends 11...0-0 12.Kg2 Rfe8.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 61<br>Sher–[[Georg Mohr (chess player)|Mohr]], Ljubljana 1995<br>Dumitrache–Biti, Zagreb 1997</ref>

The other gambit, 4...Nc6 5.Nf3 Bb4+ 6.Nbd2 f6 7.exf6 Qxf6, is much riskier, as Black weakens his kingside and does not open a diagonal for his Bc8. Black tries to take advantage of the fact White has moved his dark-squared bishop away from the queenside, leaving the b2-pawn without protection. The correct plan for White was shown by [[Evgeny Gleizerov|Gleizerov]] who played 8.e3 Qxb2 9.Be2 d6 10.0-0 0-0 11.Nb3 Qf6 12.c5! to open the a2–g8 diagonal that was weakened precisely by the gambit move 6...f6. The move 11.Nb3 is not only useful to support the c4–c5 push, but also to exchange the knight against Black's dark-squared bishop after a possible a2–a3 forcing the retreat Bb4–c5.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 47<br>[[Evgeny Gleizerov|Gleizerov]]–Bosch, Cappelle La Grande Open 1996</ref> As Lalic puts it, "I doubt if Black has a satisfactory answer to White's play in this game".<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 49</ref>

==Alekhine Variation: 3...Ng4 4.e4==
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.e4''
{{Chess diagram small
| floatright
|
|rd|nd|bd|qd|kd|bd|  |rd
|pd|pd|pd|pd|  |pd|pd|pd
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |pl|  |  |
|  |  |pl|  |pl|  |nd|
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|pl|pl|  |  |  |pl|pl|pl
|rl|nl|bl|ql|kl|bl|nl|rl
|Alekhine Variation 4.e4
}}
This variation is named after [[Alexander Alekhine|Alekhine]] thanks to his wins in the games Alekhine–Rabinovic (Baden Baden, 1925) and Alekhine–Seitz (Hastings, 1926).<ref name="Oleinikov4">Oleinikov chapter 4</ref><ref group="notes">As cited by Tseitlin (p.21), Alekhine himself stated:
<blockquote>This is considered with good reason to be White's best system against the Budapest Gambit. White hands the pawn back, but in return gains control of d5. Over the next few moves, however, he has to play with extreme precision, since otherwise his central pawn position may become the object of a successful attack by Black.</blockquote></ref> White does not try to keep its material advantage (the e5-pawn) and concentrates on establishing a strong pawn centre and space advantage. A controversial point is whether the typical black manoeuvre Bf8–b4–xc3 is advantageous for Black (as it saddles White with doubled pawns) or for White (as it reinforces his centre). Lalic thinks both, considering 6...Bb4+ to be a bad move after 4...Nxe5 5.f4 Nec6 6.Nf3,<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 105</ref> but a good one after 4...Nxe5 5.f4&nbsp;Nec6 6.Be3.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 111</ref> After 4.e4 the main line is 4...Nxe5&nbsp;5.f4 when Black has an important choice to make about where to move the Ne5. The retreat to the queenside with 5...Nec6 is considered best,<ref>Borik 1986, p. 47, citing the [[International Master|IMs]] Harry Schlüsser and Tom Wedberg</ref> while the retreat to the kingside with 5...Ng6 is probably playable.<ref name="Borik46">Borik 1986, p. 46</ref>

Taylor considers 4...Nxe5 inferior, recommending instead a rarely played idea of Richard Réti, 4...h5! (Taylor's exclamation point). Then 5.Nf3 would allow 5...Bc5, while Taylor suggests meeting 5.Be2 with 5...Nc6! and 5.f4 with 5...Bc5 with quick development compensating for the lost pawn. He considers the main line to be 4...h5 5.h3 Nxe5 6.Be3 Bb4+, with good play for Black.<ref>Taylor 2009, p.?</ref>

===Line 5...Nec6===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.e4 Nxe5 5.f4 Nec6''
{{Chess diagram small
| floatright
|
|rd|nd|bd|qd|kd|bd|  |rd
|pd|pd|pd|pd|  |pd|pd|pd
|  |  |nd|  |  |  |  |
|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|  |  |pl|  |pl|pl|  |
|  |  |  |  |bl|  |  |
|pl|pl|  |  |  |  |pl|pl
|rl|nl|  |ql|kl|bl|nl|rl
|After 5.f4 Nec6 6.Be3
}}
The knight on c6 is safer than on g6, and is well-placed as part of a general strategy to control the central dark squares. It can go to d4 while the other knight can go to c5 via a6 or d7. After 6.Nf3 Bc5 White has difficulties castling short, because the plan to exchange the dark-squared bishops with Bd3/Qe2/Be3 can be met by Bg4/Nd4 to muddy the waters.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 47<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1503216
 | title=Vaganian vs. Wedberg, Buenos Aires Olympiad 1978
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> As Lalic points out:<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 104</ref><blockquote>White can no longer castle kingside and will usually have to go the other way. However, this is rather slow and gives Black time to try to undermine the white centre. To this end Bc8–g4 often comes in handy, in order to pin the white knight on f3 against the white queen. Note that Black should wait until his opponent has wasted a tempo with Qe2.</blockquote>

The main continuation 6.Be3 controls the a7–g1 diagonal and is considered to be the best reply.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 110</ref> If Black wants to contest the c5-square for his Bf8 he can try 6...Na6,<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 118<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1503214
 | title=Rudakovsky vs. Ratner, Moscow 1945
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> but most games continue with 6...Bb4+. Here the best reply for White is controversial.<ref group="notes">While Borik does not express a preference, Alekhine considers 7.Nc3! is "much stronger than 7.Nd2, for with the knight threatening to jump to d5, Black will sooner or later be forced to exchange his important dark-squared bishop for it. The doubling of the c-pawns in these circumstances is not something White should fear." Tseitlin agrees, stating that "after 7.Nd2 Black has no difficulty at all" (see Tseitlin 1992, pp.31 & 119). On the other hand, Lalic thinks 7.Nd2! is more accurate as "White avoids the doubled c-pawns that are likely to occur after 7.Nc3, and this knight can later be deployed via the b3-square" (see Lalic 1998, p. 111).</ref>

After 7.Nc3 Black has the [[zwischenzug]] 7...Qh4+ 8.g3 Bxc3+ 9.bxc3 Qe7 so that the diagonal a8–h1 is weakened before Black develops the Bc8 to the b7-square. The queen on the e7-square is well placed to pressure the e4-pawn. However, as most of Black's pieces are on the queenside, continuing with pawn pushes like f7–f5 is probably too weakening, as Alekhine demonstrated in his game against Seitz in 1925.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 51; Tseitlin 1992, p. 119<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1440569
 | title=Alekhine vs. Seitz, Hastings 1925–26
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> So Black does best to attack with pieces, possibly with the setup b6/Nc5/Bb7/0-0-0.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 53<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1071833
 | title=Keres vs. Gilg, Prague 1937
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> In that case Tseitlin considers that with a knight on c5 the move d7–d6 should be avoided if Black has to respond to the capture Bxc5 by dxc5, because the white pawns in e4 and f4 would have too much leeway.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 33</ref>

After 7.Nd2 Qe7 8.a3 Lalic considers 8...Qxe4 should be avoided, e.g. the continuation 9.Kf2 Bxd2 10.Qxd2 0-0 11.Nf3 d6 12.Re1 gives White several tempi against the black queen.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 112<br>Potocnik–G.Hofmann, Bled 1996</ref> After the better 8...Bc5 9.Bxc5 Qxc5 10.Qf3 Lalic recommends 10...a5.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 112<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1029941
 | title=Dautov vs. Blatny, Bad Worishofen 1991
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> The introduction of the intermediate 7...Qh4+ 8.g3 Qe7 does not change Lalic's opinion, as after 9.Bg2 Na6 10.a3 Bc5 11.Bxc5 Nxc5 12.b4 Ne6 the bishop was well placed on g2 and Black experienced difficulties developing the Bc8.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 111<br>Dautov–Haas, Buehl 1992</ref> But Lalic does not mention the game [[Arturo Pomar|Pomar]]–[[Wolfgang Heidenfeld|Heidenfeld]] cited by Borik, in which Black played the advance a7–a5 to restrict the white advance b2–b4, and achieved equality after 9.Bg2 a5 10.Ne2 Na6.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 50<br>Moskalenko 2008, p. 136<br>Pomar–Heidenfeld, Enschede 1963</ref> Instead, he recommends 7...d6 8.Nf3 0-0 9.Bd3 and now the same development as in Pomar's game:<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 113</ref>
<blockquote>9...a5 and 10...Na6 deserves attention, when White's movements on the queenside are more restricted and the black knight will be able to settle on the c5-square without being kicked by the thematic b2–b4. It may appear that we have reached the same position elaborated in previous games a tempo down for Black, since he has committed his bishop to b4 and will later drop back to the c5-square instead of heading there at once. However, the white knight is less actively placed on d2 and in fact this fully compensates Black for the slight loss of time.</blockquote>

The knight on g6 puts the f4-pawn under pressure, but may be embarrassed later by the pawn thrust f4–f5. Now 6.a3, an attempt to deny squares from the Bf8 by continuing with b2–b4 or Bc1–e3, does not achieve its goal after 6...Bc5! 7.b4?! Bxg1! 8.Rxg1 0-0! 9.Qf3 d6 10.g4 a5 11.b5 Nd7 12.Ra2 Nc5 when Black's superior pawn structure and well-positioned Nc5 gives him the advantage.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 123<br>Mechkarov–Atanasov, correspondence 1955</ref> That leaves White with the choice between 6.Nf3 and 6.Be3.

The move 6.Nf3 controls the e5-square in order to prepare the push f4–f5. Unlike after 5...Nec6, White does not have to fear 6...Bc5?!, which encounters difficulties after 7.f5! Nh4 8.Ng5!, when the black knight is already in danger of being lost to Qd1–g4 or Qd1–h5.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 43; Tseitlin 1992, p. 120<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1007250
 | title=Alekhine vs. Rabinovich, Baden-Baden 1925
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}<br>Borik wrongly attributes the black pieces to [[Adolf Seitz|Seitz]] in his book, while Tseitlin and Lalic rightly note the Black player was actually [[Ilya Rabinovich|Rabinovich]].</ref> Instead Black must react quickly with 6...Bb4+ 7.Nc3 when he can adopt a normal setup with d6/0-0/Nc6/b6 or act boldly with 7...Qf6 threatening both the Nc3 and the f4-pawn.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 45<br>Chebotayev–Isayev, USSR 1948</ref> One point in favour of 7...Qf6 is that after 8.e5 Qb6 the black queen prevents White from castling short and is well placed if White castles long.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 39</ref>

The move 6.Be3 takes the a7–g1 diagonal from Black's Bf8 and may in some lines prepare the long castle. After the mandatory 6...Bb4+ White can opt for 7.Nd2 to avoid having doubled pawns, but he must be prepared to sacrifice a pawn after 7...Qe7 8.Kf2!? Bxd2 9.Qxd2 Qxe4 10.Bd3 with piece activity for the pawn deficit,<ref name="Lalic120">Lalic 1998, p. 120</ref> because the normal defence 8.Bd3? runs into 8...Qd6! and both the Bd3 and the f4-pawn are attacked.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 120<br>Lorscheid–Dunnington</ref> White does not need, however, bother too much about the doubled pawns and after 7.Nc3 Bxc3+ 8.bxc3 a peaceful Black might choose the quiet 8...b6!? followed by a normal development with d6/0-0/Bb7/Nd7/Re8/Nc5.<ref name="Borik46"/> Instead of 8...b6 a more adventurous Black could choose 8...Qe7 9.Bd3 f5!? as indicated by Borik, Tseitlin and Lalic,<ref name="Borik46"/><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 36</ref><ref>Lalic 1998, p. 122</ref> but in his more recent book Moskalenko thinks "this move complicates the game too much".<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 126<br>Golichenko–Malienko, Kiev 2007</ref> If Black is neither peaceful nor aggressive, Lalic proposes an alternative with 8...Qe7 9.Bd3 0-0 10.Qd2 and only now that Black has his king safe shall he unleash 10...f5!?, when "it is not so easy for White to meet [10...f5] as the two main responses, 11.e5 and 11.exf5, allow Black promising chances with 11...d6 and 11...Nxf4 respectively".<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 122</ref>

The Fajarowicz Variation is said to have its origins in the chess circles from [[Leipzig]], with the first important game being H.Steiner–[[Sammi Fajarowicz|Fajarowicz]] at the 1928 [[Wiesbaden]] tournament.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 60</ref><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 89</ref> In this variation, Black makes no immediate effort to regain the gambit pawn, preferring to concentrate on active piece play and tactical tricks.<ref group="notes">These tactical pitfalls include notably a Bb4+ at an annoying moment, a Qf6 with a double attack on b2 and f2, (after 1...d6 2.exd6 Bxd6) the pseudo-sacrifice 3...Nxf2 4.Kxf2 Bg3+ and 5...Qxd1 winning White's queen for two minor pieces, and a concerted attack on the d3 square with the setup Nc5/Bf5/Nb4 (once White has played e3).</ref>

The move 4.a3 allows White to avoid the annoying bishop check on b4, the also annoying knight jump to b4, and prepares Qc2 to undermine Black's knight. Both Lalic and [[Nick de Firmian|de Firmian]] consider it to be White's best move,<ref>Lalic calls it "undoubtedly the most critical line". Lalic 1998, p. 132</ref> with de Firmian assessing it as leading to a large advantage for White.<ref name="MCO-15p504">{{cite book
 | author=De Firmian, Nick
 | title=[[Modern Chess Openings]]: MCO-15
 | publisher=Random House Puzzles & Games
 | year=2008
 | page=504
 | isbn=978-0-8129-3682-7
 | author-link=Nick de Firmian
}}</ref> Lalic considers 4...b6!? to be the best answer, one point being that Qd1–c2, so effective in most of the other lines, can be met by Bc8–b7. After 5.Nd2 Bb7 6.Qc2 Lalic gives 6...Nxd2 7.Bxd2 a5! when the black bishops will be excellently placed on the b7- and c5-squares.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 139<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1299848
 | title=Timoschenko vs. Welling, Ostend 1991
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> White cannot win a piece with 5.Qd5{{chesspunc|?!}} because of the usual trick 5...Nc5{{chesspunc|!}} 6.Qxa8{{chesspunc|?}} Bb7 7.Qxa7 Nc6, trapping White's queen. Lalic recommends 6.Nf3 instead,<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 139<br>Ricardi–Perez, Olivos 1993</ref> while de Firmian continues by 5.Nf3&nbsp;Bb7 6.Nbd2 Qe7 7.Qc2 with a large advantage for White.<ref name="MCO-15p504"/><ref>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1270178
 | title=Hillarp Persson vs. Romero Holmes, Hotel Bali Stars 2003
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref>

The move 4.Nf3 develops a piece and covers the sensitive d2-square. After 4...Bb4+ 5.Nbd2&nbsp;Nc6 6.a3 Black can easily get confused by the move order. The natural 6...Nxd2 7.Bxd2 Bxd2+ 8.Qxd2 Qe7 9.Qc3 transposes in the same position as after 5.Bd2, but White can also try 6...Nxd2 7.axb4! Nxf3+ 8.gxf3 Nxe5 9.Rg1 Qe7 10.Ra3! with a strong initiative.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 143<br>Kullamaa–Starke, Correspondence 1991</ref> White can even retain his bishop with 6...Nxd2 7.Nxd2 and now Borik recommends 7...Bf8 with difficult play for Black as he is not certain to gain his pawn back.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 91</ref> To avoid these possibilities Lalic advises the move order 6...Bxd2+ 7.Bxd2 Nxd2 8.Qxd2 Qe7, but does not mention the possibility of White answering 6...Bxd2+ with 7.Nxd2. A possible improvement for Black (after 4...Bb4+ 5.Nbd2) would be 5...d5 with compensation for the pawn in all lines.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 96</ref>

The line 4.Qc2 immediately attacks the Ne4, as a retreat by Black would effectively surrender his temporary lead in development, which is the compensation for the sacrificed pawn. Black must continue to develop while trying to keep the Ne4 on its square, but that is by no means easy. Borik thinks 4.Qc2 is the move "that gives Black the most problems to solve",<ref name="Borik68">Borik 1986, p. 68</ref> but Lalic does not agree at all, stating that the reply "4...Bb4+ [....] followed by d7–d5 ensures Black a rapid development and plenty of counterplay. It is for this reason that 4.Qc2 is not on the danger list".<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 152</ref> The reply 4...Bb4+ ({{em|diagram}}) pins the white pieces before deciding what to do with the Ne4. White cannot reply 5.Bd2 as he would lose the bishop pair and Black would easily regain the e5-pawn with Nc6/Qe7/0-0/Re8. After 5.Nd2 this knight would be misplaced and would block the Bc1, so Black could open the game with 5...d5 in favourable circumstances. Best for White is 5.Nc3 d5 6.exd6 Bf5 7.Bd2 Nxd6 8.e4! Bxc3 9.Bxc3 Bxe4 when Black has regained his pawn but White has the bishop pair and possibilities of an attack on the kingside.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 84</ref>
{{clear}}

==Other possibilities==

===Line 3...Ng4 4.e3===
''1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 Ng4 4.e3''

Apart from the main lines 4.Bf4, 4.Nf3 and 4.e4, the only significant other fourth move is 4.e3 to continue by 4...Nxe5 5.Nh3 (or the other move order 4.Nh3 and 5.e3) so that the white knight starts the journey Ng1–h3–f4–d5 reach its ideal d5-square.<ref>According to Tseitlin, this idea first occurred in the game Baginskaite–Stroe, Vilnius 1986</ref> The idea with 4.e3 and 5.Nh3 was favourite of a leading Soviet coach and writer [[Mikhail Shereshevsky]], who wrote in his 1994 book ''The Soviet Chess Conveyor'' that the line was first shown to him by a strong [[Correspondence chess|correspondence]] player [[Donatas Lapienis]].{{sfn|Shereshevsky|1994|p=21}} Black has tried to prevent White's idea by the suitably strange-looking move 5...Ng6, taking the f4-square from the Nh3. Then White can develop along various setups, the most active being 6.Qh5 with the possibility Nh3–g5 in store to recycle the knight towards a more central position.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 101<br>[[Mihail Marin|Marin]]–de la Villa, Szirak interzonal 1987</ref> Black can also ignore White's intentions and concentrate on his own play by placing the Nb8 on c5, in order to put pressure on the d3-square. After 5...g6 6.Nf4&nbsp;Bg7 7.Be2 0-0 8.0-0 d6 9.Nc3 Nbd7 10.Qd2 a5 11.b3 Nc5 the position of Black's knights is secured and Black's position is similar to the Leningrad Variation of the [[Dutch Defence]] (once he has played f7–f5).<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 98–99<br>Zwikowski–Gurieli, Genting Highlands 1990<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1503226
 | title=Gurevich vs. Tisdall, Akureyri 1988
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}<br>Zayats–[[Vladimir Malaniuk|Malaniuk]], Minsk 1988</ref> White has no reason, however, to abandon the a1–h8 diagonal to Black, and he can try 5...g6 6.Bd2 d6 7.Nf4 Bg7 8.Bc3 0-0 9.Be2 Nbd7 10.Nd2 b6 and in one game White gained a minimal edge.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 98<br>[[Aleksej Aleksandrov|Aleksandrov]]–Pavlenko, Ashkhabad 1990</ref>

A few other lines have been tried, with the outcome varying from an immediate equality to a clear advantage for Black. The cooling 4.e6 avoids complications and heads for an equal endgame with 4...dxe6 5.Qxd8+ Kxd8, Black's loss of the right to castle being of no great importance since queens have been traded. If Black wants to avoid this early endgame, he can try 4...Bb4+ 5.Nc3 Bxc3+ 6.bxc3 dxe6 and now the exchange of queens would give a plus to Black, as the white queenside pawns are isolated and doubled.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 127<br>Henriksson–Wiander, Helsingborg 1991</ref> The greedy 4.f4 is weak because White neglects his development and weakens the a7–g1 diagonal.<ref>{{cite book
 | author=Pachman, Ludek
 | title=Opening game in chess
 | publisher=Routledge
 | year=1983
 | isbn=978-0-7100-9222-9
 | author-link=Ludek Pachman
}}</ref><ref>Lalic 1998, p. 129<br>Akhundov–Simonenko, [[Ashgabat|Ashkhabad]] 1990</ref><ref>Borik 1986, p. 55</ref><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 111<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1278816
 | title=Helmar vs. Krejcik, Vienna 1917
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> Black can immediately exploit this with 4...Bc5, which threatens a fork on f2 and stops White from castling kingside. Then a quick ...d7–d6 will open the centre to Black's advantage.<ref>Moskalenko 2008, p. 186</ref>

Another reasonable-looking move is 4.Qd4 as it protects the e5-pawn and attacks the Ng4. However, ''"the problem for White in the Budapest is that natural moves often lead to disaster"''.<ref>Lalic 1998, p. 129<br>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1329002
 | title=Beliavsky vs. Epishin, Reggio Emilia 1991
 | website=[[Chessgames.com]]
}}</ref> Best for Black is the gambit 4...d6 5.exd6 Nc6! 6.Qd1 Bxd6, when the natural 7.Nf3?? is an error because of 7...Nxf2! 8.Kxf2 Bg3+ winning the queen.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 18</ref> White must develop quietly with moves like Nc3/Nf3/e3/Be2, allowing Black to find active positions for his pieces with 0-0/Be6/Qe7/Rfd8, and preparing several sacrificial ideas on e3 or f2, with excellent attacking possibilities.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 20</ref> Similar to 4.Qd4 is 4.Qd5 when after 4...Nc6 White can seize the last opportunity to return to calm waters with 5.Bf4 Bb4+ 6.Nc3 which will transpose in the Rubinstein line,<ref name="Tseitlin15">Tseitlin 1992, p. 15</ref> or he can try 5.Nf3 d6 6.exd6 Be6 7.d7+ Bxd7 when Black's lead in development compensates for the pawn.<ref>Borik 1986, p. 57</ref>

===Declining the gambit===
Declining the gambit is almost never seen in master play because it promises White equality at best. After 3.d5?! Bc5 White has prematurely blocked the central position, giving the a7–g1 diagonal to Black for his bishop. In this variation Black can either play on the queenside with a plan like b5/Nb6/Bd7, or on the kingside with a plan like Ne8/g6/Ng7/f5.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 12, citing Kwiatkowski</ref> The shy 3.e3?! exd4 4.exd4 transposes into a line of the Exchange Variation of the [[French defence#Exchange Variation: 3.exd5|French Defence]] with 4...d5, but Black can also develop rapidly with 4...Bb4+ 5.Bd2 Bxd2+ 6.Nxd2 0-0.<ref name="Borik93">Borik 1986, p. 93</ref><ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 12</ref> After 3.e4? Black gains a crushing attack via 3...Nxe4 4.dxe5 Bc5 5.Nh3 d6 6.Qe2 f5 7.exf6 0-0! 8.fxg7 Re8 9.Be3 Bxe3 10.fxe3 Bxh3 11.gxh3&nbsp;Qh4+.<ref>Tseitlin 1992, p. 11, citing [[Carl Schlechter|Schlechter]]</ref><ref name="Borik94_S">Borik 1986, p. 94, citing [[Carl Schlechter|Schlechter]]</ref> After 3.Bg5?! the game Ladmann–[[Savielly Tartakower|Tartakower]] (Scarborough 1929) continued with 3...exd4 4.Qxd4 Be7 5.Nf3 Nc6 6.Qd1&nbsp;Ne4 7.Bxe7 Qxe7 8.a3 d6 9.e3 0-0 10.Be2 Qf6 11.Nbd2 Bf5 when both Tseitlin and Borik assess the position as favourable for Black.<ref name="Borik93"/><ref name="Tseitlin11">Tseitlin 1992, p. 11</ref> After 3.Nf3?! the game Menchik–Tartakower (Paris 1929)<ref>{{cite web
 | url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessgame?gid=1258247
 | title=Menchik vs. Tartakower, Paris 1929 |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref> continued with 3...e4 4.Nfd2 d5 5.cxd5?! Qxd5 6.e3 Bb4 7.Nc3 Bxc3 8.bxc3 0-0 and White has problems developing his kingside because of the potential weakness of g2.<ref name="Tseitlin11"/>
{{clear}}

==Assessment==
Nicolas Giffard summarises the modern assessment of the Budapest Gambit:<ref>{{cite book
 | author=Giffard, Nicolas
 | title=Le guide des échecs
 | publisher=Robert Laffont
 | isbn=2-221-05913-1
 | language=fr
 | page=307
 | year=1993
}}</ref><blockquote>[It is] an old opening, seldom used by champions without having fallen in disgrace. While White has several methods to get a small advantage, this defence is strategically sound. Black gets a good pawn structure and possibilities of attack on the kingside. His problems generally come from the white pressure on the d-file and a lack of space to manoeuvre his pieces.</blockquote> 

[[Boris Avrukh]] writes, "The Budapest Gambit is almost a respectable opening; I doubt there is a refutation. Even in the lines where White manages to keep an extra pawn, Black always has a lot of play for it."<ref>Avrukh 2010, p. 91</ref>

[[Kiril Georgiev]] explains the opening's lack of popularity as follows:<ref>Georgiev 2010, pp. 127–128</ref><blockquote>The pawn structure that arises from the Budapest Gambit, is basically in White’s favour. It is much more flexible for the first player, who has a number of good strategic plans. [...] Black’s pawn formation, on the contrary, is very solid, without weaknesses, but entirely defensive. It does not provide a positional foundation for any active plan. Thus the second player is restricted to piece play only.</blockquote>
