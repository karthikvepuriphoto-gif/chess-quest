The '''Tennison Gambit''' is a [[chess opening]] in which White [[gambit]]s a [[Pawn (chess)|pawn]].<ref>{{cite book |authorlink=Eric Schiller |last=Schiller |first=Eric |title=Gambit Opening Repertoire for White |chapter=Tennison Gambit |pages=171–178 |publisher=Cardoza Publishing |isbn=978-0-940685-78-9 |date=1998 }}</ref><ref>{{cite journal |url=http://www.vachess.org/news/1998_2.pdf |format=PDF |journal=Virginia Newsletter |date=1998 |pages=13–15 |number=2 |publisher=Virginia Chess Federation |department=The Gambiteer |first=Andy |last=Tejler |title=Tennison Gambit }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.365chess.com/eco/A06_Tennison_(Lemberg_Zukertort)_gambit |work=365.com |title=A06: Tennison (Lemberg, Zukertort) gambit }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chesscollection?cid=1009312 |title=Tennison gambit Collection |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref>

The opening moves begin with either the [[Zukertort Opening]]:<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessopening?eco=A06 |title=Reti Opening A06 |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref>

:1. [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. Nf3|Nf3]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. Nf3/1...d5|d5]] <br />2. e4

or the [[Scandinavian Defense]]:
:1. [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4|e4]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...d5|d5]] <br />2. Nf3

The ''[[Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings]]'' code for the Tennison Gambit is A06.

==History==
The first person to significantly research this opening was chess amateur Otto Mandrup Tennison (1834–1909).<ref name="Wall">[http://billwall.phpwebhosting.com/articles/Tennison%20Gambit.htm Wall, Bill. ''Tennison Gambit''.]</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.chessgames.com/perl/chessplayer?pid=99715 |title=Otto M. Tennison |website=[[Chessgames.com]] }}</ref> Tennison was born in Denmark, studied in Germany and moved to the United States in 1854.<ref name=OCC /> There, he played in the chess clubs of [[New Orleans]]. Many strong players picked up the idea from the first half of the 20th century.

==Analysis==
The Tennison Gambit is considered theoretically unsound, as White [[Sacrifice (chess)|sacrifices a pawn]] immediately with no solid positional [[Compensation (chess)|compensation]]. Without precise counterplay, however, Black can easily fall into a number of tactical [[List of chess traps|traps]] that leave White ahead.<ref name="lichess">{{cite web |url=https://lichess.org/study/7pODei7u/AvAXyWtz |title=Dirty Chess Tricks in Tennison Gambit: The Opening |author=((Raidmax21)) |date=2018 |work=Lichess |department=Studies }}</ref> 

After 2...dxe4 3.Ng5:
*3...Nf6 4.Bc4 e6 5.Nc3 a6 6.Ngxe4 Nxe4 7.Nxe4 b5 8.Be2 Bb7 9.Bf3 and White had the advantage in [[Evgenij Ermenkov|Ermenkov]]–Bonchev, Bulgaria 1970.
* 3...e5{{chesspunc|!}} 4.Nxe4 f5 and 4.h4 Be7 5.Bc4 Bxg5 6.Qh5 g6 7.Qxg5 f6 favor Black.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Benjamin |first1=Joel |authorlink1=Joel Benjamin |last2=Schiller |first2=Eric |authorlink2=Eric Schiller |title=Unorthodox Openings |publisher=[[Macmillan Publishers (United States)|Macmillan Publishing Company]] |year=1987 |page=79 |contribution=Tennison Gambit |isbn=0-02-016590-0}}</ref>
* 3...Bf5 and Black has the better position. A continuation might be 4.g4 Bg6 5.Bg2 h5 and Black keeps the advantage in a sharp position.
