The '''Damiano Defence''' is a [[chess opening]] beginning with the moves:
#[[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4|e4]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...e5|e5]]
#[[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...e5/2. Nf3|Nf3]] [[b:Chess Opening Theory/1. e4/1...e5/2. Nf3/2...f6|f6?]]

The defence is one of the oldest chess openings, with games dating back to the 16th century. It is a weak opening that gives a large advantage for White after 3.Nxe5. Even if White does not go for this continuation, simple {{chessgloss|development}} leads to an advantage since 2...f6 prevents the g8-knight from developing to f6 and weakens Black's {{chessgloss|kingside}}.

The ''[[Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings]]'' code for the Damiano Defence is C40 ([[King's Knight Opening]]).

The opening is named after the Portuguese chess author [[Pedro Damiano]] (1480–1544), despite the fact that he condemned it as weak and the book may actually be [[Francesc Vicent]]'s writing that he signed. In 1847, [[Howard Staunton]] wrote of 2...f6, "This move occurs in the old work of Damiano, who gives some ingenious variations on it. [[Ruy López de Segura|Lopez]], and later authors, have hence entitled it 'Damiano's Gambit'."<ref>Howard Staunton, ''The Chess-Player's Handbook'', Henry C. Bohn, 1847, p. 60.</ref> Instead, Staunton's contemporary [[George Walker (chess player)|George Walker]] more logically reserved the term "Damiano Gambit" for the knight sacrifice played by White on the third move: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 f6 3.Nxe5.<ref>Walker wrote of the knight sacrifice, "This constitutes the Damiano Gambit." George Walker, ''The Art of Chess-Play: A New Treatise on the Game of Chess'' (4th ed. 1846), Sherwood, Gilbert, & Piper, p. 236.</ref> Staunton referred to [1.e4 e5 2.Nf3] 2...Nc6, a highly respected move then and now, as "Damiano's defence to the {{chessgloss|king knight|K. Kt.}}'s opening".<ref>Staunton, p. 64.</ref>

The Damiano Defence is never seen today in top-level play. The greatest player to play the Damiano in serious master competition was [[Mikhail Chigorin]]. As noted above, he played the 3...Qe7 line in a game against Schiffers at St. Petersburg 1897. Chigorin lost his queen on move 10 ({{em|see diagram}}), but Schiffers played so weakly that Chigorin later missed a brilliant {{chessgloss|forced mate}} and only escaped when Schiffers agreed to a [[draw (chess)|draw]] in a winning position.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.xs4all.nl/~timkr/chess2/diary_12.htm|title=The Richter riddle|work=Open Chess Diary (scroll down to No. 222)|accessdate=2006-03-20}}</ref> Robert McGregor played the Damiano in a 1964 [[simultaneous exhibition]] against [[Bobby Fischer]], essaying 3...Qe7 4.Nf3 d5 5.d3 dxe4 6.dxe4 Qxe4+ 7.Be2 Bf5, and drew, although Fischer did not play the best moves.<ref>[https://chessstuff.blogspot.com/2005/04/bobby-fischer-and-damianos-defense.html  Bobby Fischer and Damiano's Defense]. Chessstuff.blogspot.com. Retrieved on 2009-04-02.</ref>

==White responses==

===3.d4 and 3.Bc4===
Black's 2...f6{{chesspunc|?}} is a weak move that exposes Black's [[king (chess)|king]], weakens their kingside and prevents Black from developing their [[Knight (chess)|knight]] to f6. The moves 3.d4 and 3.Bc4 are strong replies; [[I. A. Horowitz]] wrote (substituting algebraic notation for his [[descriptive chess notation|descriptive notation]]), "Simple and potent is 3.Bc4 d6 4.d4 Nc6 5.c3, after which Black chokes to death."<ref>{{cite book |last=Horowitz |first=I. A. |authorlink=Israel Albert Horowitz |title=Chess Openings: Theory and Practice |publisher=[[Simon & Schuster]] |orig-year=First pub. 1964 |page=227 n. 31. |isbn=0-671-20553-6 }}</ref>

The most forceful move is the knight [[sacrifice (chess)|sacrifice]] 3.Nxe5{{chesspunc|!}}<ref>''Understanding the Chess Openings'', Sam Collins, 2005, p. 28.</ref> Taking the knight with 3...fxe5? exposes Black to a deadly attack after 4.Qh5+ Ke7 (4...g6 loses to 5.Qxe5+, [[fork (chess)|fork]]ing king and [[rook (chess)|rook]], leaving Black down [[The exchange (chess)|the exchange]], though other options are worse) 5.Qxe5+ Kf7 6.Bc4+ d5! (6...Kg6{{chesspunc|??}} 7.Qf5+ is devastating and leads to [[Checkmate|mate]] shortly after) 7.Bxd5+ Kg6 8.h4 (8.d4? Bd6!) 8...h5 ({{em|diagram}}; 8...h6 is similar, except Black cannot play 9...Bxb7 because of 10.Qf5{{chessAN|#}}) 9.Bxb7! Bd6 (9...Bxb7 10.Qf5+ Kh6 11.d4+ g5 12.Qf7! mates quickly) 10.Qa5!, when Black's best is 10...Nc6 11.Bxc6 Rb8, and now White can play 12.Qxa7 with five extra pawns. [[Bruce Pandolfini]] notes that Black's opening is thus sometimes described as "the five pawns [[gambit]]".<ref name="Pandolfinip.92">[[Bruce Pandolfini]], ''Chess Openings: Traps and Zaps'', Simon & Schuster, 1989, p. 92. {{ISBN|0-671-65690-2}}.</ref> Alternatively, White can continue developing their [[chess piece|pieces]], remaining four pawns up. In either case, White has a clearly winning position.

Since taking the knight is fatal, after 3.Nxe5 Black should instead play 3...Qe7!<ref name="Pandolfinip.91">Pandolfini 1989, p. 91.</ref> (Other Black third moves, such as 3...d5, lead to 4. Qh5+! g6 5. Nxg6!) After 4.Nf3 (4.Qh5+? g6 5.Nxg6 Qxe4+ 6.Be2 Qxg6 leaves Black ahead a piece for a pawn)<ref name="Pandolfinip.91" /> 4...Qxe4+ 5.Be2, Black has regained the pawn but has lost {{chessgloss|time}} and weakened their kingside, and will lose more time when White chases the [[queen (chess)|queen]] with Nc3, or 0-0, Re1, and a move by the [[bishop (chess)|bishop]] on e2. [[Nick de Firmian]] in ''[[Modern Chess Openings]]'' analyses instead 4...d5 5.d3 dxe4 6.dxe4, when White had a small advantage in [[Emmanuel Schiffers]]–[[Mikhail Chigorin]], [[Saint Petersburg|St. Petersburg]] 1897.<ref>''Modern Chess Openings, 15th Edition'', Random House Puzzles & Games, 2008, p. 156. {{ISBN|978-0-8129-3682-7}}.</ref>

The fact that Black can only regain the pawn with 3...Qe7! shows that 2...f6? did not really defend the e-pawn at all. Indeed, even a relatively useless move like 2...a6{{chesspunc|?!}} is less risky than 2...f6? After 2...a6?! 3.Nxe5, Black could still regain the pawn with 3...Qe7 4.d4 d6, but has not weakened the kingside or prevented the {{chessgloss|king's knight}} from developing to f6.
